Heritage

A country of many rooms

Malawi is smaller than its variety. The useful thing, as a visitor, is not a costume catalogue. It is knowing which language to try, which places explain themselves, and when to put the camera down.

People and languages

Chewa
Widely in the centre and south. Chichewa is the everyday lingua franca.
Tumbuka
The north, with its own language and a strong presence around Rumphi and Mzimba.
Yao
Along parts of the lake, including Mangochi, with a long Islamic and trading history.
Lomwe
The southeast, in the tea hills around Thyolo and Mulanje.
Ngoni
Incomers of the nineteenth century, in Mzimba and Ntcheu, known for Ingoma dance.
Sena and Tonga
The Lower Shire, and the northern lakeshore, each with their own speech.

Start in Chichewa: moni, muli bwanji?, ndili bwino, zikomo. The greeting note has the rest.

What you may be invited to see

Gule Wamkulu is a Chewa masquerade, recognised by UNESCO, tied to the Nyau society. The masks are specific, the meanings are not a show, and some moments are not for outsiders. If you are asked not to photograph, that is the whole rule.

Ingoma is Ngoni dancing, athletic and drummed, closer to a public performance and still somebody’s history.

Malipenga and Beni grew on the lakeshore: brass, uniform, and drill turned into dance. You are more likely to meet them at a community day than at a hotel.

The Kungoni Centre at Mua is the place that explains Chewa art without pretending to stage it on demand.
